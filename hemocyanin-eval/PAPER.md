# Four and a half volumes per cent: an evaluation of *Copper-Based Hemocyanin Physiology in Hominids*

karagos01, October 2026

## Abstract

M. Mazgal, *Theoretical Framework for Copper-Based Hemocyanin Physiology in
Hominids* (Zenodo, 7 October 2026, DOI
[10.5281/zenodo.23209266](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23209266)) argues that
iron-based haemoglobin is an "oxidative tax" whose Fenton chemistry drives
ageing, that humans carry latent genes for copper-based hemocyanin, and that
feeding an isolated population for generations on biomass from a
metal-saturated soil matrix would epigenetically switch them over — yielding
lifespans of centuries, a superconducting nervous system and a biological
quantum computer with ¹³⁸La-doped synapses.

The question is settled by one quantity, and **neither the preprint nor any of
its five references states it**. Human arterial blood carries about **20 vol%**
oxygen. Hemocyanin carries 1.37 to 3.44 vol% in measured species and "never
exceeds 4.5 vols%" in cephalopods generally — **4.4 to 14.6 times less**. It
cannot be concentrated to close the gap, for a reason given by the co-author of
the paper the preprint's first citation garbles: a pigment dissolved in
haemolymph at transport-sufficient concentration "will yield an unbearably high
osmotic pressure".

The consequence is computable. A 70 kg adult at rest consumes 245 mL O₂/min,
which at a normal 4–6 L/min cardiac output requires an arteriovenous difference
of **4.1–6.1 vol%** — a figure that reproduces the ~5 vol% actually measured in
humans, so the arithmetic is calibrated. With hemocyanin at the published
cephalopod ceiling and a generous 75 % extraction, the same person needs
**7.3 L/min at rest**; at a measured *Octopus vulgaris* capacity, **24–72
L/min**, against a maximal human cardiac output of roughly 20–25 L/min in a
trained athlete. The proposal fails sitting still.

The genetic route fails on a precondition rather than a degree. An epigenetic
switch turns an existing gene on; **vertebrates have no hemocyanin gene** —
InterPro IPR013788 returns nothing in Vertebrata or Chordata, and the three
human tyrosinase-domain genes are TYR, TYRP1 and DCT, of which TYRP1 holds zinc
rather than copper in its active site. The preprint's only concrete molecular
bridge is denied by its own cited abstract: ceruloplasmin "plays no essential
role in the transport or metabolism of" copper and is a **ferroxidase**
regulating iron efflux. And its fourth reference attributes to copper "the
generation of superoxide and **hydroxyl radicals**" — the species the Fenton
reaction is named for, and the reason given for abandoning iron.

One finding goes the other way and is strong enough to state prominently. **A
pigment-class switch really has happened in an animal lineage** — Planorbid
snails replaced ancestral hemocyanin with secondarily evolved haemoglobin. The
preprint does not cite it. It runs in the opposite direction, the published
reason is "possibly to replace a less-efficient hemocyanin", and it is
one-way: "Once lost, hemocyanin was not regained."

## 1. What is correct, and what this evaluation is not doing

**Iron chemistry really is a source of oxidative damage.** The Fenton reaction
is real, ferroptosis is real, and iron overload is a real pathology. The
preprint's starting observation is not invented.

**Hemocyanin really is a copper-based respiratory pigment** that works in
molluscs and arthropods, and it really does have properties haemoglobin lacks,
including temperature-adapted affinity and cooperativity. The preprint is not
describing a fictional protein.

**Hemocyanin animals really do reach mammalian mass-specific metabolic rates.**
Epipelagic squid have temperature-corrected metabolic rates above mammals of
similar size. This is the strongest argument available to the proposal and §4
below takes it seriously rather than dismissing it.

What this evaluation does not do is adjudicate the ageing claims. "Lifespans of
hundreds of years", a "superconducting nervous system" and a "biological quantum
computer" are not measured here; §6 says what can be said about their form and
stops. The oxygen calculation is sufficient on its own, and it comes first
because it does not require any of the harder questions to be settled.

A note on counting: the preprint uses "epigenetic" and its forms **eight
times**, while "methylation", "histone" and "chromatin" each occur **zero**
times, and it reports **no measured quantity of any kind** — the only numbers in
it are section numbers, years, reference page ranges, a licence version and
"138".

## 2. The number the preprint does not give

| fluid | vol% O₂ | vs human |
|---|---|---|
| human arterial blood | **20.00** | 1.00× |
| *Cancer magister* | 3.44 | 5.8× |
| *Octopus dofleini* | 3.06 | 6.5× |
| *Nautilus pompilius* | 2.30 | 8.7× |
| *Octopus vulgaris* | 1.37 | 14.6× |
| cephalopods, published ceiling | 4.50 | 4.4× |

Four to fifteen times less oxygen per unit volume. And the gap is structural,
not a matter of formulation. Haemoglobin is packed inside erythrocytes at
330–340 mg/mL, where it contributes nothing to plasma osmolarity; hemocyanin is
dissolved in the haemolymph, so its concentration is capped by colloid osmotic
pressure and by viscosity. From van Holde, Miller & Decker (2001) — which is the
paper the preprint's first citation points at by venue while pointing at a
different one by author and year:

> "a low molecular weight monomeric protein dissolved in the hemolymph is not
> efficient for oxygen transport. If its concentration in the hemolymph is high
> enough to transport a significant amount of oxygen, it will yield an
> unbearably high osmotic pressure."

That is the mechanism of the gap, stated by a co-author of the work the preprint
cites for hemocyanin's "efficiency, and thermodynamic stability" — two phrases
that occur zero times in the paper the citation actually resolves to.

## 3. What that costs the circulation

A 70 kg adult at one metabolic equivalent consumes 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min = **245 mL
O₂/min**. At a resting cardiac output of 4–6 L/min the blood must give up:

| cardiac output | required a-v difference |
|---|---|
| 4.0 L/min | 6.12 vol% |
| 6.0 L/min | 4.08 vol% |

Human arterial blood is 20 vol% and venous about 15, so the measured
arteriovenous difference at rest is around 5 vol%. The calculation reproduces
it, which is the check that the arithmetic describes the system it claims to.

Now substitute hemocyanin. Extraction cannot be complete — unloading needs a
partial-pressure gradient — so sweep it:

| capacity | extraction | delivered | cardiac output needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.50 | 75 % | 3.38 vol% | **7.3 L/min** |
| 4.50 | 25 % | 1.12 vol% | 21.8 L/min |
| 1.37 | 75 % | 1.03 vol% | 23.8 L/min |
| 1.37 | 25 % | 0.34 vol% | **71.5 L/min** |

At the most generous published capacity and the most generous plausible
extraction, resting cardiac output has to rise above the normal resting range.
At a measured octopus capacity it has to reach 24–72 L/min, which exceeds
maximal human cardiac output. And exertion multiplies the requirement by 8 to
20: at the ceiling, 8× resting needs **58 L/min** and 20× needs **145 L/min**.

So the proposal does not fail at the margins. It fails at rest, by a factor of
more than one, before any of the claims about ageing or cognition are reached.

## 4. Why squid are not a counterexample

Epipelagic squid do achieve metabolic rates above similar-sized mammals, which
is the fact a defender of this proposal should reach for. They do it by moving
much more fluid much faster, and the people who measured it name the cost:

> "The constraint on power output in squids is clearly not oxygen uptake, but
> **oxygen transport in the blood**"

That is, the binding limit on a squid is exactly the quantity the preprint
proposes humans adopt. The supporting physiology is correspondingly extreme: a
Bohr coefficient of −1.2 to −2.3 against the human −0.35 to −0.5, venous
saturations around 14 %, effectively no aerobic scope, and a life lived close to
the oxygen limit. The largest hemocyanin animal, the colossal squid at nearly
500 kg, is an ambush predator whose metabolic rate is orders below an active
squid of the same clade — mass is available to hemocyanin animals, sustained
aerobic power is not.

A mechanistic footnote, tagged `[A]` and offered as explanation rather than
evidence: haemoglobin carries four O₂ per 64.5 kDa tetramer, about 16 kDa of
protein per O₂, while hemocyanin carries one O₂ per binuclear type-3 site in a
subunit of order 50–100 kDa. That is 3–6× the protein mass per oxygen, the same
bracket as the measured capacity gap, which is what one would expect if the
difference is largely stoichiometric and partly osmotic. The capacity
measurements in §2 are the evidence; this is only the reason.

## 5. The genetic route fails on a precondition

An epigenetic mechanism changes which of an organism's genes are expressed. It
cannot express a gene the lineage does not possess. So the proposal has a
precondition that is a matter of record:

| query | result |
|---|---|
| hemocyanin family IPR013788 in Vertebrata (7742) | **0** |
| same, restricted to Chordata (7711) | **0** |
| human genes with the tyrosinase domain hemocyanins grew from | TYR, TYRP1, DCT |
| metal in the TYRP1 active site | **zinc**, not copper |

There is nothing to unlock. TYRP1 is not even a latent oxygen binder — "Because
zinc ions, in contrast to copper ions, are not redox-active".

The preprint's one concrete molecular bridge is ceruloplasmin, which it calls
"the fundamental human protein for copper transport in the blood", "structurally
related to invertebrate hemocyanins". Its own cited review opens:

> "Ceruloplasmin is a serum ferroxidase … Despite the need for copper in
> ceruloplasmin function, **this protein plays no essential role in the
> transport or metabolism of this metal.**"

Twenty-eight of that review's 112 references concern iron and none concern
hemocyanin; its structural comparisons go to ascorbate oxidase, laccase, Fet3p
and hephaestin. The databases agree: ceruloplasmin is a multicopper oxidase with
a cupredoxin fold, the hemocyanins carry a tyrosinase binuclear type-3 centre,
and the domain intersection is empty. Different superfamily, different fold,
different class of copper site — and a physiological role that runs through iron
efflux, the opposite of the direction needed.

And the premise that copper is the gentler metal is contradicted by the
preprint's fourth reference:

> "it is this property of copper that also makes it potentially toxic because
> the transitions between Cu(II) and Cu(I) can result in the generation of
> superoxide and **hydroxyl radicals**."

Hydroxyl radicals are what the Fenton reaction is named for. Both metals are
redox couples, which is why both can carry oxygen and why neither is inert. The
same review mentions no epigenetics at all — "epigenet" occurs zero times in it,
against the gloss that calls it a "baseline framework for understanding the
epigenetic adaptation of organisms to extreme environmental metal
concentrations".

## 6. The real precedent, and three findings the preprint could have used

This section exists because the content audit of this deposit's references
searched for support, not only for error, and found some — none of it cited by
the preprint.

**A pigment-class switch really has occurred.** Planorbid snails "have replaced
the ancestral (blue) hemocyanin as an oxygen-carrier with secondarily evolved
hemoglobin". So the idea that a lineage can change respiratory pigment is not
fantasy. Three problems: the direction is hemocyanin → haemoglobin; the
published reason is "possibly to replace a less-efficient hemocyanin", which is
this repository's own finding arriving from the other side; and it is one-way —
"Once lost, hemocyanin was not regained."

**Hemocyanin genes persist after a switch.** *Biomphalaria glabrata*, which uses
haemoglobin, still carries two hemocyanin-like genes, and the same cell type —
the rhogocyte — makes hemocyanin in blue-blooded snails and haemoglobin in
red-blooded ones. This is the closest real analogue to the preprint's "latent
genes", and it holds within molluscs, where the gene exists to be retained.

**Hemocyanin expression is environmentally regulated.** Embryonic hemocyanin is
"highly upregulated by ambient O₂ deprivation, up to 10-fold", and in some
insects expression is restricted to the embryo. So regulation of this specific
pigment by environmental oxygen is documented — in animals that have the gene.

None of the three reaches across to a lineage with no hemocyanin gene at all,
and none of them changes the capacity arithmetic. But they are what a defender
should cite, and it is worth recording that they exist and that the preprint
does not use them.

The full content audit of all five references, with verbatim evidence and a
record of how far each work could be read, is in
[`reference-audit`](https://github.com/karagos01/reference-audit). Two of the
five actively contradict the preprint, one does not resolve as printed at all,
and the fifth is a self-citation whose target does not contain the mechanism it
is cited for: "copper" occurs once in the phytomining preprint, in a bullet
heading whose body is entirely about gold, and copper appears in no line of that
paper's material budget — whereas iron does.

## 7. What is not measured here

"Lifespans of hundreds of years", a "superconducting nervous system",
thermoregulatory independence, a "biological quantum computer" with ¹³⁸La-doped
synapses, and the framing of "blue blood of aristocracy" as real biochemistry
rather than a social expression — none of these is measured, and no attempt is
made.

Two observations about their form, which is all that is available. None is
stated with an endpoint, a timescale or a comparison, so none is falsifiable as
written. And none has a carrier in the reference list: not one of the five
references concerns neurophysiology, tissue conductivity or lifespan.

This deposit and its predecessor are also the two places in this author's
seventeen where the proposal is an intervention on people — here, an isolated
human population fed for generations on metal-loaded crops in order to change
their blood. That is a difference in kind from a motor controller or a rendering
architecture, and it is recorded as such without further comment.

## 8. Limitations

No blood was drawn, no gene was sequenced and no organism was studied. The
capacity figures are from the published literature on four species plus a
review ceiling, and they vary between species, with temperature and with
nutritional state; the conclusion is stated across the whole measured spread
rather than at any one value.

Provenance is tagged in `constants.py`: `[P]` the preprint's own assertions,
`[V]` verified against a named source, `[A]` an assumed range swept rather than
asserted. Cardiac output, extraction fraction, VO₂max multiple and the protein
masses per oxygen are all `[A]`.

The extraction fraction is the softest assumption. Tissues do not extract a
fixed fraction; extraction rises with demand and falls with flow, and a
hemocyanin-based circulation would presumably evolve toward the high end. The
sweep runs to 75 %, which is above the human resting figure of about 25 %, so
the calculation is already generous to the proposal — and it still fails at
rest.

The TYRP1 and InterPro results are database queries made while writing this. A
negative database result is weaker than a proof of absence: it shows that no
vertebrate hemocyanin gene has been annotated, not that none could exist. Given
that the α-subclass the molluscan hemocyanins derive from is absent from
chordates, the two lines of evidence agree, but the claim is scoped to what the
annotation shows.

## 9. Data and code availability

`constants.py` holds every value with its provenance tag and source, and no
other script defines one. `oxygen.py` reproduces §§2–4 and `genetics.py` §5 and
part of §6; `genetics.py` is a record of database results and quotations rather
than a computation, kept as a script so it appears in `results.log` beside the
arithmetic. `run_all.sh` runs everything offline in about a second.
